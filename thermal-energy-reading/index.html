1. Conduction

Thermal energy transfer through matter, without bulk flow of the material.

Partner A reads · Partner B listens
📖 Read together

What happens inside a solid?

Place one end of a metal spoon in hot water. After a short time, the handle becomes warm even though the metal itself has not travelled along the spoon. This is conduction: thermal energy is transferred through a material from a region of higher temperature to a region of lower temperature. The difference in temperature produces a temperature gradient.

In a solid, atoms occupy average positions in a lattice and vibrate about those positions. When one region is heated, its particles have greater average kinetic energy. Interactions with neighbouring particles transfer energy through the solid. The atoms vibrate more strongly but do not collectively move from the hot end to the cold end. This is why conduction does not require bulk movement of the solid.

Why are metals usually good conductors?

Metals have delocalised electrons that can move throughout the metal. Electrons transfer energy between regions of different temperature and exchange energy with the vibrating lattice. This additional mechanism makes many metals particularly effective thermal conductors. Materials such as wood and plastic usually have a much lower thermal conductivity, which is why they are often used as handles or insulation.

In IB Physics, the rate of conduction generally increases with the temperature difference, cross-sectional area and thermal conductivity of a material, but decreases as the length of the conduction path increases.

Watch out: The atoms in a metal spoon are not flowing from the hot end to the cold end. It is the energy that is transferred through the material.

Discuss · Explain it to your partner

A explains · B challenges

A metal spoon becomes hot at the handle when its other end is placed in hot water. Explain how energy travels through the spoon, and why a wooden spoon behaves differently.

Use the words particles, electrons and thermal conductivity in your explanation. Partner B: ask, “Does the metal itself move?”

A strong explanation should include: Energy is transferred along the spoon because particles near the hot end have more kinetic energy and interact with neighbours. In metals, delocalised electrons also transfer energy through the lattice. The solid does not flow along the spoon. Wood has fewer mobile charge carriers and lower thermal conductivity, so energy transfers along it much more slowly.

2. Convection

Thermal energy transfer through the large-scale movement of fluids.

Partner B reads · Partner A listens
📖 Read together

Why do fluids circulate?

Unlike conduction through a solid, convection involves the movement of the substance itself. Convection occurs in fluids, meaning liquids and gases, because their particles can move past one another. Heating a region of a fluid usually makes it expand. If its mass remains approximately constant while its volume increases, its density decreases.

A warmer, less-dense region surrounded by cooler, denser fluid experiences a net upward buoyant force and tends to rise. Cooler, denser fluid moves down to replace it. This establishes a convection current, transferring energy by the movement of warmer and cooler fluid. Conduction between neighbouring particles also occurs, but it is the large-scale circulation that defines convection.

Where do we see convection?

Consider air near a radiator. The air beside the radiator warms, expands and generally rises. Cooler air moves towards the radiator to replace it, creating circulation through the room. A similar process contributes to sea breezes: land often warms faster than the sea, encouraging warm air to rise over land and cooler air from the sea to move inland.

The Earth's mantle is often described in terms of mantle convection. Although it is predominantly solid rock, over geological timescales it can deform and flow extremely slowly. This is very different from the rapid circulation of air or water in the classroom model.

Watch out: “Heat rises” is imprecise. It is the warmer, generally less-dense fluid that rises. The same fluid cannot form a convection current if it cannot move.

Discuss · Explain it to your partner

B explains · A challenges

Explain how heating the bottom of a container of water can produce a circulating current. Why would the same process not occur in a rigid metal block?

Build your explanation around temperature, density, buoyancy and bulk movement. Partner A: check which parts actually move.

A strong explanation should include: Fluid at the bottom warms and usually expands, reducing its density. Buoyancy causes this warmer fluid to rise as cooler, denser fluid sinks and replaces it, creating a circulating current. In a rigid metal block the material cannot flow in this way, so energy is transferred mainly by conduction instead.

3. Thermal radiation

Energy transfer by electromagnetic radiation — no material medium required.

Partner A reads · Partner B listens
📖 Read together

How can energy cross empty space?

Energy from the Sun travels through the near-vacuum of space to Earth. This cannot be explained by conduction or convection through space, because these processes require matter. Instead, energy is carried by electromagnetic radiation. This is called thermal radiation when the radiation is emitted because an object has a temperature.

Every object above absolute zero emits electromagnetic radiation. At ordinary everyday temperatures, much of this emission is infrared. Electromagnetic radiation can also be described as packets of energy called photons. Unlike conduction and convection, thermal radiation does not require a medium to transfer energy.

What controls the energy emitted and absorbed?

As an object becomes hotter, it emits more energy per second as thermal radiation. The emission spectrum also shifts, with a greater proportion of the emitted radiation occurring at shorter wavelengths. At very high temperatures some emitted radiation may be visible, as in a glowing filament.

Objects can both emit and absorb radiation. The net transfer depends on the radiation they emit and receive. Surface properties matter: a surface with high emissivity emits thermal radiation effectively. An ideal black body has the maximum possible emissivity of 1. Colour alone is not a reliable guide to infrared emissivity.

Watch out: Radiation is not only emitted by visibly glowing objects. A person, a desk and a cold glass all emit thermal radiation, although the amounts and wavelengths differ.

Discuss · Explain it to your partner

A explains · B challenges

The Sun warms Earth even though most of the space between them is almost a vacuum. Explain the mechanism involved, and why conduction and convection cannot be the main mechanisms across space.

Try to include electromagnetic radiation, photons, vacuum and energy transfer. Partner B: ask whether only very hot objects radiate.

A strong explanation should include: The Sun emits electromagnetic radiation which carries energy as photons through near-empty space. It can cross a vacuum without material particles. Conduction requires interactions between particles and convection requires moving fluid, so neither can transport thermal energy through the vacuum of space. All objects above 0 K emit radiation, not only objects that glow visibly.
