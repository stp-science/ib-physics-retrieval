02 / MOLECULAR EVIDENCE · 8 MINUTES

What makes a greenhouse gas?

Investigate how different molecules interact with radiation using PhET: Molecules and Light.

What happens inside a molecule?

1. Infrared reaches the molecule. A greenhouse-gas molecule can absorb certain frequencies of infrared radiation.

2. The molecule gains energy. When it absorbs an infrared photon, its atoms vibrate more: bonds can stretch or bend. The photon has transferred its energy to the molecule.

3. The energy can move on. Collisions pass energy to other molecules, warming the air. Molecules can also emit infrared in any direction, including back towards Earth.

Only certain frequencies are absorbed. The photon must have the right energy for a change in the molecule’s energy level. N₂ and O₂ do not significantly absorb Earth’s infrared in this way.

Your investigation

  1. Open the simulation. Choose Visible and turn the light source on.
  2. Test CO₂, H₂O, CH₄, N₂ and O₂. Record whether photons pass through or are absorbed, and whether the molecule changes.
  3. Switch to Infrared. Repeat for the same five molecules. Keep the radiation type the same when comparing molecules.
  4. Observe an absorption and subsequent emission. What happens to the molecule? Does the emitted photon always travel in the original direction?
  5. Use your observations to explain why CO₂ contributes to the greenhouse effect but N₂ does not significantly do so.

NO₂ ≠ N₂O — and the light source is a model.

PhET includes nitrogen dioxide, NO₂. The key greenhouse gas in your source table is nitrous oxide, N₂O, which this simulation does not offer. Do not substitute NO₂ for N₂O. The infrared source demonstrates a molecular interaction: for the greenhouse effect, connect it to infrared emitted by Earth’s surface. It does not mean all incoming sunlight is infrared, or that every infrared wavelength is absorbed.
